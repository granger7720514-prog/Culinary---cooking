Pasta & cheese making

Learn fresh pasta basics, cheese styles, and simple fresh cheese methods.

🍝

Fresh egg pasta dough

  1. Use 200 g flour and 2 large eggs for a small batch. Make a mound, add eggs, and mix gradually until shaggy.
  2. Knead on a clean surface 8–10 minutes until smooth; cover and rest 30 minutes.
  3. Roll thin with a pasta machine or rolling pin, dust lightly with flour, then cut into noodles or shape.
  4. Cook in boiling water until tender, usually 2–4 minutes depending on thickness. Do not taste raw dough; flour and raw eggs can carry harmful germs.
🌾

Semolina pasta & shapes

Semolina-and-water dough is a common egg-free style. Add water gradually to semolina, knead until firm and smooth, rest covered, then roll or shape. Match thickness so pieces cook evenly. Keep fresh pasta chilled if not cooking promptly; dry only with a tested method.

🧀

Cheese families

  • Fresh: ricotta, paneer, cottage cheese.
  • Soft-ripened: Brie and Camembert.
  • Washed-rind: aromatic soft cheeses.
  • Semi-hard and hard: Gouda, cheddar, Parmesan.
  • Blue: veined cheeses such as Stilton.
  • Brined and stretched-curd: feta and mozzarella.
🥛

Cheese making: fresh paneer

Use pasteurized milk and clean utensils. Heat 2 quarts milk slowly to steaming, stirring so it does not scorch. Remove from heat; add 3–4 tablespoons lemon juice gradually until curds separate from yellowish whey. Rest 10 minutes, strain through clean cheesecloth, rinse briefly with cool potable water, gather and press under a weight until firm. Refrigerate promptly and use within a few days. This fresh cheese is not shelf-stable.

🧫

Making cultured or aged cheese

Use pasteurized milk, food-grade cultures and rennet, sanitized equipment, and a tested recipe with exact temperatures, acidity, salt, aging time, and humidity. Small changes can affect safety and texture. Do not use raw milk or improvise aging conditions. Refrigerate finished cheese as the tested recipe directs.

🧼

Cheese room & storage

Clean and sanitize food-contact equipment according to product directions. Keep milk cold until use, wash hands, prevent cross-contamination, label batches, and refrigerate fresh cheese at 40°F (4°C) or below. Avoid raw-milk cheese; unpasteurized dairy can carry harmful germs.

Home wine & beer making

Fermentation basics with sanitation, temperature control, and pressure safety.

Use a tested home-brewing recipe and food-grade equipment. Sanitize everything that touches the cooled wort, juice, or fermenting beverage. Never seal active fermentation in a bottle; carbon dioxide pressure can break glass.
🍺

Beer: basic brew day

  1. Choose a tested extract or all-grain recipe and prepare clean, food-grade equipment.
  2. Make wort following the recipe; boil hops on the schedule it gives, then cool promptly to the yeast’s recommended range.
  3. Transfer to a sanitized fermenter, add brewing yeast, and fit an airlock so gas can escape.
  4. Keep it at the recipe’s fermentation temperature. Use a hydrometer or the recipe’s validated checks to confirm fermentation is complete.
  5. Package only when fermentation is complete, using the recipe’s measured priming sugar and pressure-rated bottles. Store and chill as directed.
🍷

Wine: basic fermentation

  1. Choose a tested grape or fruit-wine recipe and clean, sanitize, and inspect all food-grade equipment.
  2. Sort and prepare fruit or juice as directed; add yeast and any recipe-specified nutrients.
  3. Ferment in a suitable vessel with an airlock at the recipe temperature; keep notes and avoid unnecessary oxygen exposure.
  4. When fermentation and clarification are complete, follow the recipe’s stabilization and bottling steps.
  5. Never guess at preservative or sulfite amounts. Use tested quantities, label bottles, and store them correctly.
🧽

Sanitation & pressure checks

  • Clean first, then use a brewing sanitizer according to its label.
  • Use an airlock during fermentation; do not tightly cap an actively fermenting batch.
  • Do not bottle until fermentation is complete and stable by the recipe’s measurement method.
  • Use bottles designed for carbonation and the exact priming amount in a trusted recipe.
  • Discard bottles that leak, crack, or show unexpected pressure; protect eyes and hands when opening homebrew.
Penn State Extension: beer and fermentation · Penn State Extension: wine production
⚖️

Responsible home production

Follow local rules for making, possessing, and sharing alcoholic beverages. Keep alcohol away from children and anyone who should not drink; label homemade bottles clearly and never serve a beverage if you are unsure it fermented or stored correctly.

Cooking methods

Learn what the heat is doing and when to use each method.

🔥

Dry heat

Roasting, baking, broiling, grilling, and sautéing use hot air, radiant heat, or a hot pan to brown food.

  • Roast: cook uncovered in an oven; turn dense vegetables for even browning.
  • Bake: cook with surrounding oven heat; preheat and use the rack and pan the recipe specifies.
  • Broil: cook close to intense top heat; watch continuously because food browns fast.
  • Sauté: use a preheated pan and a small amount of fat; avoid crowding.
  • Grill: preheat grates and use separate plates for raw and cooked food.
💧

Moist heat

Water, stock, or steam transfers heat gently and helps keep food tender.

  • Simmer: small, steady bubbles; useful for soups and braises.
  • Boil: vigorous bubbles; useful for pasta and blanching.
  • Steam: cook above boiling water under a lid; keep food out of the water.
🍲

Braising & stewing

Combination methods brown food first, then soften it slowly with liquid and covered heat—ideal for tougher cuts and hearty vegetables.

  • Braise: sear first, add liquid partway up the food, cover, and cook gently until tender.
  • Stew: cut into even pieces, brown in batches, cover with liquid, then simmer until tender.
  • Keep a gentle simmer; a hard boil can toughen meat and break vegetables apart.
🥘

Searing, pan-frying & deep-frying

Searing creates a browned surface in a hot pan; it adds flavor but does not seal juices in or make the inside safe.

  • Pat food dry, preheat the pan, add a thin layer of oil, and leave room between pieces.
  • Pan-fry in more fat than sautéing, turning to brown each side.
  • Deep-fry in a stable pot with a thermometer; do not overfill, and keep water away from hot oil.
  • Finish thick cuts to their safe internal temperature and verify with a thermometer.
🌫️

Smoking

Smoke adds flavor while heat cooks the food. Use a covered smoker or grill and monitor its air temperature with a separate thermometer.

  • Keep smoker air between 225°F and 300°F (107–149°C).
  • Keep raw meat cold until it goes into the smoker; do not partially cook and finish later.
  • Check the food's internal temperature with a clean thermometer; smoking time alone is not proof of safety.
  • Refrigerate smoked leftovers within 2 hours.
🧂

Curing, brining & marinating

Salt, acids, and curing ingredients change flavor and texture. These processes do not automatically make food safe to eat without cooking.

  • Brine or marinate in the refrigerator, never on the counter.
  • Use a tested, reputable recipe for curing; measure curing salt exactly. Never substitute curing salts by guesswork.
  • Do not taste uncooked cured meat or assume home-cured fish is parasite-free.
  • For shelf-stable curing, fermentation, or drying, follow a validated food-preservation process precisely.
♨️

Other useful methods

Match the method to the food and thickness; always finish with safe temperatures where required.

  • Poach: gently cook submerged in liquid below a simmer.
  • Blanch & shock: briefly boil vegetables, then chill in ice water to stop cooking.
  • Pressure cook: follow the appliance directions; don't overfill and release pressure safely.
  • Slow cook: thaw meat first and start the cooker promptly.
  • Sous vide: use reliable time-and-temperature tables and chill/store promptly.
  • Air-fry: hot circulating air crisps food; leave space around pieces and check doneness.
  • Microwave: cover, stir or rotate, allow standing time, and verify reheated leftovers reach 165°F.
  • Ferment or pickle: use tested proportions, clean equipment, and a validated recipe; improvised preservation can be unsafe.
🥚

Eggs and custards

Gentle, even heat keeps eggs tender. For food safety, cook egg dishes to 160°F (71°C), unless using pasteurized eggs in a no-cook recipe.

  • Scramble over low to medium-low heat, stirring slowly.
  • Use a thermometer for quiche, casseroles, and custards.
  • For poaching, use barely simmering water.
🌾

Grains and legumes

Most grains absorb measured liquid; beans soften through slow hydration and cooking.

  • Rinse grains when the package directions call for it.
  • Soak dry beans if desired, then simmer in fresh water until fully tender.
  • Never cook dried kidney beans only in a slow cooker; boil them first.
🍰

Baking basics

Baking depends on measured ingredients, oven temperature, and timing. Preheat fully and avoid opening the oven repeatedly.

  • Measure flour by weight when possible.
  • Use the pan size in the recipe; it affects cooking time.
  • Check doneness with the recipe's cues, not time alone.
🥢

Stir-frying

Fast cooking over high heat in a wok or wide skillet.

  • Cut ingredients into small, even pieces; dry them and mix the sauce before heating.
  • Preheat the pan, add a little high-heat oil, then cook aromatics briefly.
  • Add food in batches, starting with firm ingredients and finishing with quick-cooking items and sauce.
  • Keep food moving and do not crowd the pan, which can make it steam.
🍳

Griddling

A flat hot surface browns foods with direct contact.

  • Preheat a clean griddle or heavy skillet; lightly oil the surface.
  • Pat food dry and place pieces with room between them.
  • Let the first side brown before turning; scrape and clean the surface between batches.
🔥

Pan-roasting

Start with stovetop browning, then finish thicker pieces in the oven.

  • Preheat an oven-safe skillet and pat the food dry.
  • Sear the food on the stovetop until browned on the first side; turn it.
  • Move the skillet to a preheated oven and cook until the center reaches its safe temperature.
  • Use an oven mitt and rest the food before serving.
🍤

Shallow-frying

Cook food in a shallow layer of hot fat, turning to brown both sides.

  • Use a heavy pan and enough oil to come partway up the food.
  • Heat oil steadily, add dry food away from you, and leave space between pieces.
  • Turn with tongs; drain on a rack or paper towels and check safe internal temperature for meat or seafood.
  • Keep water away from hot oil and never leave it unattended.
📦

Cooking in parchment (en papillote)

A sealed parchment packet gently steams fish, vegetables, or chicken in its own moisture.

  • Cut food into similarly sized pieces and season with a little liquid, herbs, or oil.
  • Place on parchment, fold and crimp the edges into a sealed packet, leaving room for steam.
  • Bake on a sheet pan; open carefully away from your face because hot steam escapes.
  • Check the thickest piece with a thermometer when cooking meat or fish.
🫙

Confit

Slow, gentle cooking in fat or syrup softens ingredients; it is not automatically shelf-stable preservation.

  • Season small, evenly sized pieces and place them in a heavy oven-safe pot.
  • Cover with the recipe's fat or syrup and cook at a low, steady temperature until tender.
  • Cool and refrigerate promptly; follow tested storage guidance and never store home confit at room temperature.
🍋

Flambé

A brief, controlled flame burns off alcohol vapors and adds aroma; it does not make a dish alcohol-free.

  • Use a wide, stable pan away from cabinets, towels, and other flammables.
  • Turn off the burner before adding measured liquor; never pour from the bottle near a flame.
  • Ignite only if you know how to do so safely, keep a fitted lid nearby to smother flames, and never carry a flaming pan.
  • Skip the flame step if unsure; simmer the sauce instead.
🌬️

Dehydrating

Controlled warm airflow removes moisture from foods.

  • Use a tested recipe, clean equipment, and uniform thin slices for even drying.
  • Pre-treat produce when the recipe calls for it and arrange pieces in a single layer.
  • Use the dehydrator's recommended temperature and verify doneness using trusted preservation guidance.
  • Cool fully, package airtight, and store as the tested recipe directs.

Knife skills

Start with a stable board, a sharp knife, and a controlled grip.

Meat & seafood

Buy, store, prepare, and cut proteins safely before you cook.

🥩

Beef: trim, slice & portion

  • Keep raw beef refrigerated at 40°F or below; freeze what you will not use soon. Store sealed on the lowest shelf to contain drips.
  • Use a separate clean board. Pat steaks dry before searing. Trim excess hard fat with short, controlled cuts, keeping the blade angled away from your hand.
  • For strips, cut across the visible muscle fibers to make bites more tender. For stew, cut into even cubes so they cook evenly.
  • Separate whole cuts from ground beef: ground beef must reach 160°F; steaks and roasts 145°F with a 3-minute rest.
🐟

Fish: check, portion & store

  • Choose fish that is cold and properly iced or refrigerated. Keep it cold on the trip home.
  • If using within 2 days, refrigerate at 40°F or below; otherwise wrap tightly and freeze.
  • Rinse only if needed and pat dry. Remove pin bones with clean tweezers, checking with fingertips along the fillet.
  • To portion, use a sharp flexible knife and smooth strokes. For skin-on fillets, start at the tail and guide the blade between flesh and skin.
  • Cook finfish to 145°F, or until opaque and separates easily; vulnerable people should avoid raw or undercooked seafood.
🦐

Shellfish & seafood

  • Keep raw seafood separate from ready-to-eat foods and cooked seafood. Wash hands, boards, knives, and counters after contact.
  • Store live shellfish according to seller instructions in a clean, ventilated container; do not seal live shellfish airtight or submerge in fresh water.
  • Discard cracked shellfish. Cook clams, mussels, and oysters until shells open; discard any that remain closed.
  • Never eat shellfish harvested from a closed or unapproved area.
  • Thaw seafood in the refrigerator, cold water in a sealed bag, or microwave if cooking immediately.
🍗

Poultry: safe prep & cutting

  • Keep raw poultry in a leakproof container on the lowest refrigerator shelf; use within 1–2 days or freeze.
  • Do not wash raw poultry; splashing can spread germs. Pat dry with disposable towels if needed.
  • Use a dedicated board. Remove wing tips or portion joints by cutting through the joint, not bone.
  • Wash hands and sanitize the work area after raw poultry. Cook every part to 165°F.
🐖

Pork & lamb

  • Keep sealed and chilled; avoid letting raw juices touch other food.
  • For chops or roasts, trim with the knife tip pointing away. Cut across fibers when serving slices.
  • Whole pork and lamb cuts: 145°F plus a 3-minute rest. Ground pork and lamb: 160°F.
  • Slow-cook shoulder or shank with braising liquid until fork tender, while still checking safe handling and temperature.
📦

Storage map

Keep the refrigerator at 40°F (4°C) or below; place raw proteins below cooked and ready-to-eat foods.

  • Raw poultry, ground meat, and fresh sausage: 1–2 days refrigerated.
  • Beef, pork, lamb steaks/chops/roasts: 3–5 days refrigerated.
  • Fresh fish: if using within 2 days, refrigerate at 40°F or below; otherwise wrap tightly and freeze.
  • Freeze in leakproof, labeled packages. Thaw in the refrigerator, cold water, or microwave.
  • See the full FoodSafety.gov storage chart.
⚠️

Ceviche, gravlax & raw seafood

Lime juice, salt, and sugar change seafood texture and flavor but do not make raw fish equivalent to cooked fish. Thorough cooking is safest. If serving raw fish, FDA advises choosing fish previously frozen to reduce parasite risk; freezing does not kill every harmful germ. Keep seafood cold and people at higher risk should avoid raw or lightly cooked seafood.

FDA guidance for selecting and serving seafood safely

Raw protein workflow

  1. Prepare produce and ready-to-eat items first.
  2. Take only the protein you need from the refrigerator.
  3. Use a separate board and utensils; wash hands before touching anything else.
  4. Never put cooked food on a plate that held raw meat or fish.
  5. Wash with hot soapy water, then sanitize food-contact surfaces according to label directions.

Food safety, allergies & illness

Simple habits that protect everyone at the table.

Use a food thermometer. Color, texture, and clear juices cannot reliably confirm that food has reached a safe temperature.
🧼

Clean, then sanitize

  • Wash hands with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds before cooking and after handling raw meat, seafood, eggs, or trash.
  • Wash boards, knives, counters, and utensils with hot soapy water after each use.
  • Sanitize food-contact surfaces using a product labeled for food-contact use and follow its label. Cleaning removes soil; sanitizing reduces germs.
  • Keep raw meat and its juices away from ready-to-eat food. Use separate boards or wash between tasks.
  • Rinse produce under running water; do not use soap or bleach on food.
🌡️

Temperature & storage

  • Keep refrigerator at 40°F (4°C) or colder and freezer at 0°F (-18°C).
  • Refrigerate perishables within 2 hours, or within 1 hour when air temperature is above 90°F (32°C).
  • Thaw in the refrigerator, cold water, or microwave—not on the counter.
  • Keep hot food hot (140°F / 60°C or above) and cold food cold (40°F / 4°C or below).
🌿

Food allergies

In the U.S., the nine major allergens are milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy, and sesame.

  • Read every ingredient label each time; formulations can change.
  • Prevent cross-contact: wash hands, clean surfaces and tools, and keep allergen ingredients separate.
  • Ask guests directly about allergies and never assume an ingredient is safe.
  • Food intolerance is different from an allergy. For a severe or uncertain allergy, follow the person's medical plan and consult a clinician.
🦠

Foodborne illness

Contaminated food can carry bacteria, viruses, parasites, or toxins. Symptoms may include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, stomach cramps, and fever.

  • Bacteria: Salmonella, Campylobacter, E. coli, Listeria, and C. perfringens.
  • Virus: norovirus and hepatitis A.
  • Parasites: Toxoplasma, Giardia, and Anisakis.
  • Toxins: Staph toxin and botulinum toxin. Cooking may not destroy toxins already formed.
  • Seek medical advice for severe symptoms, bloody diarrhea, high fever, dehydration, or illness in a higher-risk person.
🥩
